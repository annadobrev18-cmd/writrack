## Oil Price Volatility: A Growing Concern for Banks
The recent warning from Bank of America (BofA) that oil volatility could force commercial banks to abandon their 'look-through' policy has sent shockwaves through the financial sector. This policy, which allows banks to essentially ignore the market value of certain assets when calculating their regulatory capital requirements, has been a cornerstone of banking regulation for years. However, with oil prices experiencing unprecedented levels of volatility, banks may be forced to rethink their approach.

### Historical Context: The Evolution of the 'Look-Through' Policy
The 'look-through' policy was first introduced in the aftermath of the 2008 financial crisis, as a way to provide banks with more flexibility in managing their regulatory capital requirements. By allowing banks to ignore the market value of certain assets, such as oil prices, the policy aimed to reduce the volatility of banks' balance sheets and prevent a credit crunch. However, with oil prices experiencing extreme fluctuations in recent years, the policy has come under scrutiny.

#### Oil Price Volatility: A Key Driver of Banking Policy Shift
The oil price volatility has been driven by a combination of factors, including geopolitical tensions, supply and demand imbalances, and the ongoing COVID-19 pandemic. As a result, oil prices have experienced significant fluctuations, with prices ranging from below $20 per barrel to over $70 per barrel in the past year alone. This level of volatility has made it increasingly difficult for banks to accurately predict their regulatory capital requirements, leading to a reevaluation of the 'look-through' policy.

## Implications of Abandoning the 'Look-Through' Policy
If commercial banks are forced to abandon the 'look-through' policy, it could have significant implications for the banking sector. One of the main consequences would be an increase in regulatory capital requirements, as banks would be required to hold more capital against their assets. This could lead to a reduction in lending, as banks may be less willing to take on new credit exposures.
